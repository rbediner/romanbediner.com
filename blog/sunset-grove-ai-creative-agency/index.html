How to Build an AI Creative Agency That Works for You

The decisions, roles and handoffs behind Maya.

Sunset Grove needed a sustained marketing service. Building Maya meant defining the agency’s jobs and making the work travel between AI systems.

Sunset Grove needed more than a stream of attractive posts. Its vacation homes in Wake Forest are listed on Airbnb and Vrbo. The marketing job is to give the right guests a reason to come together there, then turn that idea into creative work worth their attention.

That is the work of an agency: understanding the audience, shaping a campaign, producing it, checking it and learning what to do next. Maya was built to take on that service with AI, rather than leave the business coordinating a collection of disconnected tools. For another business, the starting point is the same: define the responsibility the agency should take on before choosing its AI tools.

Two views make the idea easier to explore. The organization chart shows who owns the work. PULSE, the animated illustration below it, shows how that work can move. Maya is the broader agency product; neither graphic is proof of commercial results.

Follow Sunset Grove on Instagram and Facebook to see the creative work as it develops. As AI technology advances, these channels offer a way to follow the work itself, alongside the ideas explored here.

Meet the creative agency

Maya is the client-facing Chief Marketing Officer. Sixteen defined employee roles report directly to Maya, covering strategy, creative production, independent review, publishing, research and growth.

These are responsibilities, not sixteen separate models. Claude, Codex and Gemini are shared execution resources behind the team. The business should be able to discuss a campaign with Maya without deciding which model or worker to ask next.

Sell the reason for the trip

Define the marketing job before choosing the tools. For Sunset Grove, the operating brief gives the agency a useful direction: sell occasions and shared experiences, rather than mattresses, empty rooms and photographs of sofas. The combined stay can accommodate a group of 13, so the creative needs to help the right guests see how staying together could fit their trip.

Consider a family gathering around a wedding. A useful campaign would connect the occasion to the stay: space for the group, time together between events and a clear path to learn more. The local research, images and copy need to support that idea. This is an illustration of the agency's assignment, not a reported campaign result.

The documented weekly plan included 17 placements for organic Instagram and Facebook. The intended path runs from that content to the website and then to Airbnb or Vrbo listing interest. Each step needs its own measurement. A listing click cannot be reported as a booking, and a popular post cannot be reported as revenue.

Turn that responsibility into a brief the agency can use: who the campaign is for, what it should help them understand, which facts and assets it may use, and what a finished result must include. Someone still has to decide what to make, check it and use the response to improve the next week. The build needs to make those responsibilities clear.

Build the agency the client would expect to hire

The project's original product definition is unusually direct: Maya should be the creative agency the client works with. Showing the client the agency's internal machinery would not satisfy that brief.

Sunset Grove should be able to talk with Maya about strategy, ask how marketing is performing, look at the work and request a change. Maya should bring a recommendation and explain the tradeoff. The business should not have to discover which AI worker is waiting for another worker, or move instructions between them.

An October 19 project record makes the gap concrete: the engine had made, independently accepted and scheduled all 17 posts, yet the documented assessment was that the client experience still failed. Work moving through a pipeline did not give the client the agency relationship they wanted.

That distinction shaped the product around six client needs: discussing strategy, understanding system health, judging content quality, interpreting results, having a real conversation and reviewing or changing creative. It also explains why adding another status dashboard would miss the point.

The product brief describes one responsive application centered on Maya. Its scope connects audience and event research, campaign recommendations, creative briefs, production, quality checks, scheduling, performance review and experiments. The client sees the choices that need their judgment. Routine coordination stays with the agency.

Use the client experience as an acceptance test. Can the business discuss a campaign, review the work and request a change through one agency relationship? If the client still has to relay instructions between workers, more generated content will not fix that problem.

Give the AI employees actual jobs

The organization chart is easier to judge when each role has a clear job. Here is the documented roster behind Maya.

All 16 report to Maya. The Operations Manager coordinates handoffs rather than becoming a second management hierarchy.

This roster goes beyond a proposed organization chart. The October 27 deployment record reports the employee procedures and bounded CMO management layer installed in production, with a live Maya response that could identify the roles and retrieve the copywriter’s working procedure. That record documents a specific part of the build. It does not prove that all 16 responsibilities run autonomously from end to end.

Give each role a responsibility that can be checked. A researcher establishes whether a local recommendation is accurate. A copywriter connects it to the audience. A creative reviewer judges the finished asset against its brief. A publishing manager makes sure the accepted version is the one that goes out.

For each role, specify the inputs it needs, the result it must return and who accepts that result. Claude, Codex and Gemini provide execution capacity behind those responsibilities. The roster does not mean every post requires 16 model calls.

The difficult part is getting the work to travel

Adding several AI systems creates coordination problems that a single prompt never has to solve. They need the same property facts and current brief. They need to know which asset version they are changing. A handoff needs to survive a delayed worker, a failed quality check or an edit that arrives after scheduling.

The AIs building the system ran into coordination problems too. The architecture record describes four rejected attempts to update shared code in an hour and a code-reconciliation attempt that broke Drive synchronization. Its design calls for separate working branches and a shared production ledger, so workers can make changes without losing track of the common job.

Without clear coordination, the person who wanted an agency becomes its project manager.

The design uses a shared message bus to carry assignments, acknowledgments and handoffs. In ordinary terms, it gives the workers a common way to pass work and report what happened. A separate production record connects a publishing slot to its brief, worker, creative version, review and publishing evidence.

Imagine Sunset Grove asks Maya to replace the opening image in a video. The agency has to identify the right video, route the edit to its producer and keep an outdated version from going out. The replacement then needs review. If the post was already scheduled, the change must reach that same publishing record rather than create a duplicate.

The client made one reasonable request. Behind it sit version control, routing, review and publication checks. Those are workflow requirements, not a claim that every recovery path has been demonstrated. They explain where much of the engineering effort goes when an AI tool becomes an agency service.

A useful operating rule is that the worker producing an asset cannot approve its own run. A separate reviewer needs the brief, the asset and the acceptance criteria, with authority to reject it. Approval belongs to a particular version. Changing that version means checking the replacement.

Keep the property recognizable

Set the creative boundaries before production. For Sunset Grove, a beautiful image is a bad advertisement if the guest arrives to find a different room. The creative rules begin with real property photography. AI can help produce the marketing without inventing a kitchen, relocating windows or redesigning the property. Named local destinations need authentic, appropriately sourced imagery too.

This is where shared business knowledge becomes practical. The agency needs current property facts, approved assets, creative standards and prior decisions before it starts another piece of work. The client should not have to repeat the same correction to three different AI systems.

Quality review then has to connect those facts to the finished asset. Is the claim accurate? Does the opening make the campaign idea clear? Can someone read the text on a phone? Does the caption belong with the image? Has the accepted version survived the handoff to publishing?

Make those checks part of accepting the work, not a correction the client has to request afterward. Producing the file is only one part of the service.

Learn from the marketing, without inventing the result

The intended learning loop links performance to the next brief. Observe what happened, form a hypothesis, try a change and measure again. Keep enough context to distinguish a promising signal from an established lesson.

The same discipline applies to business outcomes. Reach, engagement, website visits and listing clicks can help the agency decide what to investigate. Claiming increased bookings requires booking evidence. Claiming revenue requires revenue evidence. Neither comes from an animated interface or a completed production job.

Maya was built to take on the work of a creative agency for a small business. The records include installed employee procedures, a management layer and a historical completed production cycle; they do not justify declaring the entire service fully autonomous or its commercial results proven.

For another business, start with one complete campaign rather than trying to automate an entire agency at once. Define the brief, assign the work and name a separate reviewer. Decide in advance what that reviewer must see before accepting the result, and which decisions still require a person’s approval.

Then test a revision, not just a clean first run. Ask for a change and check that the right version reaches the next step. Record the corrections and coordination the person still has to do; a polished output alone will not tell you whether the agency is taking work off their hands.

That is a more useful test of an AI creative agency than its agent count. Can the business get good work back, make a change and understand what happened next, without managing the machinery itself? Build toward that service, and expand only as the work earns trust.

The implementation examples in this article come from dated project records. They describe what was documented at those points, not an independent audit of the agency’s current operation.

Have a business workflow worth rebuilding?

Start with the work the business needs done, then decide where AI can take responsibility. The useful outcome is a service people can rely on, not a larger collection of AI tools.
