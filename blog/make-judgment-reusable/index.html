Make judgment reusable

Keep the reasoning behind the review

When your team corrects AI work, preserve the decision behind the edit. Give the next person and the next workflow a better place to begin.

A useful AI draft often ends with a manager making it useful. Someone removes the promise the business cannot keep, notices the customer constraint the model missed, or changes the recommendation because the evidence does not support it.

The immediate work gets better. Then the next request arrives, and the same correction happens again.

For a CEO or business owner, that is an opportunity to improve how the company learns. The judgment people apply during review can become an operating asset: a clear standard, an example with an explanation, or a decision that the next person and the next AI workflow can find.

Keep the reason behind the edit

Consider an illustrative sales team using AI to prepare proposals. A salesperson asks for a draft. The owner changes the delivery date, removes an unsupported savings claim, and adds a dependency on the customer's data. The final document is good enough to send.

If the team saves only that final document, the useful reasoning is easy to lose. Was the date wrong because capacity was unavailable, because approval was missing, or because the customer had not supplied something? A future draft can imitate the wording while repeating the mistake.

Save the decision in language another person can apply: delivery dates require confirmation from the delivery lead; savings claims need an identified source; work dependent on customer data must say what is needed before the timeline begins. Keep the original context beside the rule so people can recognize where it applies.

This takes more care than fixing a sentence. It also gives that correction a chance to improve the next proposal, the onboarding handoff, and the conversation between sales and delivery.

Review is business work

The risk of polished but unfinished work is already visible in research. In a September 2025 survey of 1,150 full-time US desk workers, BetterUp Labs and Stanford Social Media Lab reported that 40% had received what the researchers called workslop in the previous month. Respondents reported roughly two hours to resolve an incident. These are survey findings, not a measured cost estimate for your company.

The problem can survive a better-looking draft. A proposal can read smoothly and still commit the company to something it cannot deliver. Someone has to connect the words to the facts, the commercial relationship, and the people who will do the work.

A CHI 2025 study of 319 knowledge workers found an association between greater confidence in generative AI and less reported critical thinking. The authors also describe a shift toward verification, integration, and stewardship of the task. The study concerns self-reported behavior; it does not establish that AI inevitably weakens people's judgment.

My recommendation is to give that stewardship an explicit place in the workflow. Name who accepts the work, what they are checking, and which decisions they can make. Give them a way to record a correction that deserves to become a shared standard.

Build a small decision record

The AI Daily Brief's October 30 discussion of turning decisions into precedent prompted this question of what a company retains. The practical approach I recommend is to start with one recurring responsibility. For the proposal team, a short decision record can capture the situation, the choice, the reason, and the person with authority to change it. Link it to an approved example and, where helpful, a rejected example explaining the difference.

A useful record might say: this customer needs a launch date, but delivery capacity is unconfirmed. Sales may prepare an estimated sequence. The delivery lead must approve the committed date. An owner-approved exception applies to this customer only. Review the general capacity rule when the staffing plan changes.

That last distinction matters. An exception should remain an exception until someone decides otherwise. Feeding every accepted document back into a system as a universal example can quietly turn a negotiated concession into the company's default offer.

Keep current standards in one place your team can use, with access suited to the information. A small business can begin with an existing shared document. The important work is maintaining a trustworthy current decision, its scope, and its owner. A larger knowledge platform can wait until the need is clear.

Test whether the standard travels

A saved rule has value when it changes a later result. Give the next proposal writer, whether a person using AI or an automated workflow, the current standard before drafting. Then inspect whether the draft handles the delivery commitment correctly.

Try an ordinary case and an exception. Include a case with missing information. The system should know when it can prepare work and when a decision still belongs to someone else. Reproducing the approved example word for word tells you little about a new customer with different constraints.

Ask the reviewer to record whether the same correction was needed, whether a new exception emerged, and whether the final proposal was accepted. Keep checking important claims against their actual sources. A collection of prior decisions can itself become stale or wrong.

Use those observations to decide what to improve. If repeated date promises disappear but unsupported commercial claims remain, the next change is specific. If the team keeps overriding the rule for good reasons, reconsider the rule. Avoid measuring success by how many instructions have accumulated.

Keep room for a new answer

Making judgment reusable comes with a tradeoff. A business needs consistency, but customers, capacity, and strategy change. A system that reproduces yesterday's decisions perfectly can still be wrong for today's situation.

Venkatesh Rao's Cognitive Mercantilism offers a useful broader provocation: cheaper intelligence may create new demand and possibilities. That is an argument about how to think about abundance, not proof of any company's future results.

For an owner, the practical implication is to preserve room for a new question. Use past decisions to explain the business, then ask what has changed. Let people challenge an inherited standard, identify an outdated assumption, and propose an alternative before the company repeats an old answer at greater speed.

Reserve fresh human judgment for commitments with meaningful consequences and for situations the existing standard does not cover. Clear routine decisions can be delegated within agreed limits. The authority to change those limits should stay visible.

Start with one correction

At your next review, choose one correction you have made before. Ask the responsible person to turn it into a short, usable decision record. Put it where the next task begins, then check whether it helped on the next different case.

The AI Project Manager guide covers visible commitments and ownership. The Agentic Fleet Build Guide explains the shared knowledge and controls behind a team of AI employees. Those resources provide a practical next step without requiring you to redesign the company around a new tool.

Last week's attention-budget article asked how much human work an AI responsibility consumes. This adds a learning question: when your people apply that attention, what does the business retain?

If the same correction keeps returning to your desk, bring that recurring decision to a conversation. It is a concrete place to start building a company that learns from the work it already does.
